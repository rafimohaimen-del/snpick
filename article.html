Why a shipping crisis in the Red Sea ends up on your grocery bill

Longer routes mean higher freight and insurance — and consumers pay last. The whole supply chain, in one place.

M
Mosharaf Sep 26, 2026 · Updated 8:40 AM · 8 min read
Key takeaways
  1. 1 Rerouting around Africa adds 10–14 days to an Asia–Europe voyage.
  2. 2 Extra fuel, freight and war-risk insurance are paid by importers first, consumers last.
  3. 3 Cooking oil, wheat and industrial inputs feel it most; rice is hit indirectly.

Over the past year, shipping through the Red Sea has been disrupted again and again. In the headlines it reads as a distant security crisis — but its effects reach wholesale markets thousands of miles away within weeks.1

Why does a detour cost so much?

When ships avoid the Suez Canal and sail around the southern tip of Africa, it isn't only time that grows: fuel, crew wages and insurance premiums all rise. Container freight indices have swung several-fold in this period.2

Shipping lines pass those costs to importers within a single contract cycle. Importers, in turn, price them into goods that already sit on thin margins.

Where do consumers feel it?

  • Cooking oil and wheat — directly import-dependent, so the effect is fast.
  • Garment inputs — longer lead times raise the risk of losing orders.
  • Rice — hit indirectly, through transport and fertilizer costs.

Our question is simple: how much of this extra cost is fair, and how much is opportunistic? Putting that calculation in public is the point of this piece.3
